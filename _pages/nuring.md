The NURing is a wearable haptic feedback device developed as part of my doctoral research at Northwestern University. Worn on the index finger, it delivers precise kinesthetic cues through controlled fingertip deflection driven by a tendon-based actuation mechanism. Unlike vibrotactile devices that rely on skin-level buzz patterns, the NURing communicates directional guidance by physically displacing the fingertip — a modality that remains perceptible without visual or auditory attention. This makes it particularly well-suited for navigation and guidance tasks where the user's eyes and ears are otherwise occupied.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/nuring/heroShot.png" caption="Figure 1. The camera-enabled NURing, as worn on the index finger, showing the tendon routing and actuation housing." width="100%" class="img-fluid rounded mx-auto d-block" %}

The device's mechanical design centers on a compact actuation module mounted on the dorsal side of the finger. A set of tendons route along the finger and terminate at a thimble-like contact surface on the fingertip pad. By selectively tensioning individual tendons via miniature BLDC motors, the system can deflect the fingertip in discrete directions — up, down, left, and right — with sufficient resolution to convey turn-by-turn navigation cues. The mechanism is calibrated to operate within individually-measured active range of motion thresholds, ensuring that cues are felt as clear and distinct without causing discomfort during extended wear.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/nuring/deviceOverview.jpg" caption="Figure 2. Components of the NURing." width="100%" class="img-fluid rounded mx-auto d-block" %}

A key design goal was making the device usable without sighted assistance — both in terms of wearing it and interpreting its output. Pilot studies have demonstrated the ability of the device to serve as a guidance tool for both environmental navigation as well as fingertip interaction guidance (e.g., elevator controls).

The on-going NURing project has produced several peer-reviewed publications and a pending patent covering the tendon-driven actuation approach. The broader research situates the NURing within a human-centered design framework: engineering performance is measured not only by mechanical precision, but by how meaningfully the device extends what people can do in the world without depending on sight.
